A guide to the lives behind the numbers

There is far more suffering in the world than most people ever see.

Every year, humans raise, confine, catch, and kill animals on a scale that is difficult to hold in mind. The largest groups are often the least visible. The quality of their lives is measured poorly—or not at all.

This atlas tells that story from the evidence outward: who can experience, how many lives are involved, what makes those lives go badly, what remains unknown, and what a better future could become.

The moral circle begins with experience.

The case for animal welfare does not depend on animals thinking like humans. It begins with a simpler question: can life feel good or bad from the inside?

The scientific answer reaches far beyond the animals people know best. The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness reports strong scientific support for conscious experience in mammals and birds. It finds at least a realistic possibility in every vertebrate group—including fishes, reptiles, and amphibians—and in many invertebrates, including octopuses, crabs, shrimp, and insects.

That means the boundary of moral concern cannot sensibly stop at our species, at warm-blooded animals, or at creatures whose expressions are easy for humans to read. Where there is a subject who can feel pain, fear, comfort, frustration, or pleasure, there is a life that can go better or worse.

Once experience is present, an animal is not merely a unit of production, a population member, or a weight of biomass. There is someone whose time can be good or bad.

Then the scale changes the picture.

A harm affecting one life matters. A system that repeats it across tens of billions of lives is one of the central facts of the modern world.

Humans slaughter more than 80 billion land animals for farming each year. Chickens dominate that total: in 2021, roughly 74 billion were killed for meat. That is about 202 million a day, or 140,000 in an average minute.

≈ 140,000 chickens are slaughtered for meat worldwide.

The land total is only the beginning. Fishcount estimates that 130 billion farmed finfishes were slaughtered in 2022, with a range of 86–180 billion. Its peer-reviewed estimate for wild capture is 1.1–2.2 trillion fishes per year on average from 2000 to 2019.

These figures come from different kinds of evidence. Land-animal totals mostly begin with country reports collected by the UN Food and Agriculture Organization. Fish counts must be modeled from records reported in tonnes. The precision changes from category to category. The order of magnitude does not: human food systems affect sentient lives by the hundreds of billions and trillions.

A death count is only the outline.

Welfare is the texture of the life before the count ends: the intensity of an experience, how long it lasts, and how often it occurs.

The best available global estimate says that three quarters of farmed land animals are in factory farms. At any given time, that is around 23 billion animals living in systems built to produce animal products efficiently at enormous scale.

The harms are not abstract. They include injury and disease; bodies selected to grow faster than their bones and organs can comfortably support; cages and stocking densities that prevent movement and natural behavior; chronic hunger in breeding animals; heat stress, fear, isolation, rough handling, transport, and painful killing methods.

Modern broiler chickens illustrate how production efficiency can be purchased with experience. Their growth rates have increased by more than 400% since the 1950s, and they now reach slaughter weight in five to six weeks. Rapid muscle growth contributes to lameness and skeletal pain. Quantitative work summarized by Our World in Data found that moving to slower-growing breeds could reduce time in disabling pain by 67% and excruciating pain by 80%.

The Welfare Footprint Project makes this logic explicit. Counting animals tells us prevalence. Measuring hours spent in pain at different intensities begins to describe welfare. A system can harm more animals, harm each animal more severely, keep them in that state longer—or do all three at once.

The largest figures are the least visible.

Land animals are counted as individuals. Fisheries are usually counted by weight. A tonne is useful for commerce and almost useless for understanding welfare.

Fishcount exists to reverse that abstraction. It translates fisheries and aquaculture tonnage into estimated individuals. The result moves fish from the margins of the picture to its numerical center: farmed finfish alone may outnumber all slaughtered land animals, while wild-caught fish outnumber them many times over.

Capture and slaughter can involve exhaustion during pursuit, crushing in crowded nets, decompression injuries, exposure to air, and prolonged death by asphyxiation or chilling. The animals disappear inside terms such as “landings,” “stock,” and “harvest,” but the experiences happen to individuals.

Sea Around Us shows that even the biomass record is incomplete. Its catch reconstructions add discards, small-scale fishing, and unreported catch that official landings miss. The individual welfare record is thinner still.

Beyond human custody lies the largest blind spot.

Conservation asks whether a population or species survives. Welfare asks how life is going for each animal inside that population.

Wild animals face disease, parasites, hunger, injury, temperature extremes, competition, predation, and the loss of offspring. They also play, form bonds, explore, find comfort, and pursue what they need. The question is not whether nature is simply good or bad. The striking fact is that humanity has barely begun to measure the balance.

Wild Animal Initiative describes wild-animal welfare science as the study of sentient wild animals’ lived experience and quality of life. Its research agenda spans disease ecology, nutrition, injury, stress physiology, behavior, life history, climate, and positive welfare. It also asks how roads, pollution, fishing, habitat change, light, noise, fires, and wildlife management alter individual lives.

This is a major change in perspective. A forest can gain biodiversity while some individuals suffer. A population-control program can achieve its ecological target through a more or less painful method. A species can be abundant while many of its members live badly. Population health and individual welfare overlap, but neither can substitute for the other.

There is no global welfare census for wild animals. The basic tools—validated indicators, remote monitoring, life-course data, and models that connect ecology to experience—are only now being assembled. Wild-animal welfare is not a small solved problem. It is an enormous unmeasured one.

A better future could be much better—not merely less bad.

Suffering is ancient. Treating it as morally invisible is a choice.

The near future can count animals as individuals, measure what they experience, replace the worst systems, and make severe suffering rarer. A wider moral circle can bring aquatic animals, invertebrates, wild animals, and unfamiliar minds into decisions that currently ignore them.

The longer future is more open. Sentience Institute studies how societies expand the set of beings they take seriously. The Qualia Research Institute asks whether valence—the positive or negative character of experience—can be understood with scientific precision. The Hedonistic Imperative imagines biotechnology eventually replacing suffering with gradients of well-being across sentient life. Herbivorize Predators carries that ambition into one concrete proposal for redesigning predation.

The common idea is larger than any one proposal: civilization can judge progress partly by the quality of the experiences it creates and permits. Technology could amplify exploitation and generate suffering at unprecedented scale. It could also free abundance from animal pain, make invisible lives legible, and extend humane concern beyond the limits imposed by evolution.

Powerful intelligence should notice every subject of experience—not only the beings who can speak, pay, vote, or appear in a dataset.
